\subsection{Landslide susceptibility modeling}
\label{sec:related:landslide}

Statistical and machine-learning approaches to landslide susceptibility have been the subject of extensive review \citep{reichenbach2018review, merghadi2020ml}. The field has progressively moved from bivariate and weights-of-evidence approaches to logistic regression, random forests, gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, CatBoost), and more recently to deep neural networks. \citet{brenning2005spatial} established spatial cross-validation as a methodological best practice for landslide modeling, demonstrating that random hold-out validation systematically overestimates generalization error due to spatial autocorrelation between nearby training and test pixels. Object-oriented approaches \citep{stumpf2011oo} and patch-based convolutional models have refined the spatial feature representation, but the fundamental requirement of hundreds to thousands of labeled events per study area remains a binding constraint. Recent benchmarks comparing classical and ensemble ML algorithms \citep{merghadi2020ml} report $F_1$ scores in the 0.75--0.90 range when $N>200$, but performance degrades sharply for small inventories. Most of the field operates within a single basin or contiguous region; cross-basin transfer is studied less frequently and is typically framed as out-of-sample testing rather than as a problem requiring methodological adaptation. \revtext{Inventories augmented with InSAR-detected deformation \citep{chen2023insarinventory} and multi-decadal dynamic susceptibility mapping \citep{ma2025metalsm} illustrate how richer labels and temporal structure are entering the field, which makes the question of how transfer is evaluated more, not less, pressing.}

\subsection{Transfer learning in remote sensing}
\label{sec:related:transfer}

Transfer learning by fine-tuning a model pre-trained on data-rich source domains is the de facto strategy for label-efficient remote sensing \citep{tuia2016domain}. In landslide applications, \citet{ghorbanzadeh2022landslide} demonstrated that ImageNet-pretrained convolutional networks fine-tuned on a few hundred local samples outperform train-from-scratch baselines, confirming that representations learned on natural images contain transferable low-level features. However, fine-tuning a deep network with $K<50$ target labels typically results in catastrophic over-fitting unless aggressive regularization or early stopping is applied. Furthermore, the ``concat-source'' variant---pre-training on a union of source basins, which is the baseline we adopt---implicitly assumes that all source domains are equally relevant to the target, a hypothesis that is rarely tested explicitly and that fails when the source pool spans heterogeneous environmental conditions\revtext{; it also weights each source domain by its sample count, so that a single large inventory can dominate the pooled representation, a confound we isolate in Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting}}. Recent work has begun to formalize cross-region transferability through spatiotemporal deep-learning frameworks that integrate environmental context \citep{luo2026transferable}, transferable landslide segmentation via universal adapters \citep{wei2024universaladapter}, cross-resolution landslide mapping \citep{ng2025crossresolution}, and adversarial domain adaptation \citep{zhang2025awda}; semi-supervised event detection from multi-temporal optical imagery offers a complementary path for sparse-inventory settings \citep{deijns2024semisupervised}. However, these approaches target dense segmentation from imagery, and landslide-specific cross-basin transfer on point inventories with rigorous spatial validation remains an open question.

\subsection{Domain adaptation}
\label{sec:related:da}

Domain adaptation (DA) seeks to learn classifiers that generalize across distribution shifts by leveraging both labeled source data and unlabeled target data. The Domain-Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) of \citet{ganin2015dann} is the canonical method: a feature extractor is trained simultaneously to (i) discriminate the class label and (ii) confuse a domain classifier through a Gradient Reversal Layer, encouraging the learned representation to be domain-invariant. Variants based on Maximum Mean Discrepancy \citep{long2015dan} and Wasserstein distance have followed. In remote sensing, DA has been applied to multi-source land-cover classification, image segmentation, and scene recognition, with mixed success \citep{tuia2016domain}: performance gains are typically substantial when source domains are abundant and labeled target data are completely absent, but degrade when only a handful of source domains are available or when small numbers of labeled target samples are accessible. The latter regime is precisely our setting\revtext{, and our results below show that adversarial alignment yields no advantage at $K=1$ and a small one at $K=5$ when the source pool has ten domains}.

\subsection{Meta-learning}
\label{sec:related:meta}

Meta-learning frames the data-scarcity problem as one of \emph{learning to learn}: the model is trained on a distribution of related tasks so that adaptation to a new task from few examples requires only a small parameter update. Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML; \citealt{finn2017maml}) is the canonical second-order method, computing gradients through inner adaptation steps, but it is computationally expensive and unstable in practice. First-order approximations---FOMAML, which drops the second-order term, and Reptile \citep{nichol2018reptile}, which replaces query-loss-driven updates with averaged inner-step displacements---are more practical. Theoretical work \citep{raghu2020rapid} suggests that MAML's success can be largely attributed to feature reuse rather than rapid weight adaptation, which we revisit in our adaptation-curve analysis (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

In remote sensing, meta-learning has been applied to few-shot land-cover classification \citep{russwurm2020metalearning}, achieving 70--90\% accuracy with $K=5$ samples per class on EuroSAT and Sen12MS benchmarks, and to satellite image classification more broadly \citep{tseng2022timm}. \revtext{For landslide susceptibility, \citet{chen2022metalsm} segmented two large study areas into blocks with distinct landslide-inducing environments and meta-learned an intermediate representation from which block-specific models are adapted with very few samples, demonstrating few-shot adaptation to unseen blocks; \citet{ma2025metalsm} demonstrated temporal meta-learning for dynamic susceptibility mapping at a single site (Hong Kong), decomposing yearly subtasks and achieving fast adaptation in 5 samples and 5 inner steps. Our work differs from both in the unit of transfer and in the evaluation: the tasks are whole watersheds that differ in climate, lithology and inventory source rather than blocks or years of one area; the targets are evaluated under spatially disjoint, buffered and leakage-controlled validation with partition-level inference; and the meta-learners are compared against domain-adaptation, basin-balanced pooled and classical baselines on identical partitions and support draws. The first two studies established that meta-learning can adapt susceptibility models from few samples; the present one asks how much of that advantage survives rigorous evaluation when the new task is a different watershed.}

\subsection{\revtext{Validation bias in spatial prediction}}
\label{sec:related:validation}

\revtext{The upward bias of random cross-validation on spatially autocorrelated data is well established in ecology and soil science \citep{roberts2017cv, ploton2020spatialvalidation}. \citet{meyer2021aoa} formalised the distinction between interpolation within the training extent and extrapolation beyond it, and showed that the choice of cross-validation design determines which of the two is being measured. \citet{wadoux2021spatialcv} argue, conversely, that spatial cross-validation is not the right tool for estimating map accuracy over a defined area, for which a probability sample is needed; we therefore use spatial cross-validation to compare methods under matched conditions, not to certify the accuracy of any map. Beyond the fold design, several other conventions inflate reported skill: evaluation on balanced test sets at a fixed threshold ignores the extreme class imbalance of operational mapping; pooling correlated repetitions (episodes within a fold, seeds within a region) understates uncertainty; negatives drawn far from positives create a steep-versus-flat problem easier than the real one \citep{abdelkader2025nonlandslide}; and feature selection on the pooled data of all study areas lets information from the test areas enter the model before evaluation. Each of these has been discussed in isolation; their joint effect on a cross-basin transfer benchmark, and the question of which transfer claims survive their removal, is the subject of this paper.}
