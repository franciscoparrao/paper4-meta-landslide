Remote sensing has reshaped landslide susceptibility mapping by delivering globally consistent inputs: Sentinel-1 C-band SAR products for surface-deformation and amplitude features \citep{poggi2026sentinellandslide}, Sentinel-2 multi-spectral composites for surface cover and vegetation indices, MERIT and Copernicus 30~m DEMs for topographic and hydrologic derivatives, and WorldClim 2.1 bioclimatic surfaces for environmental context. These data, harmonized to a common grid, feed the dominant paradigm of fitting a machine-learning classifier to a per-basin inventory of labeled events---a workflow that produces strong results when local training data are abundant \citep{reichenbach2018review, merghadi2020ml, ma2025metalsm}, and that sits within the broader trajectory of deep-learning adoption across photogrammetric and remote-sensing tasks documented by \citet{heipke2020deeplearning}. However, the practitioner facing a \emph{newly-studied} basin confronts a data-scarcity bottleneck: digitizing a usable inventory typically requires expert geomorphological mapping over hundreds of events, an effort that can take months and is rarely funded for individual watersheds. As a result, basins with sparse inventories ($N<50$ mass-movement records) cannot benefit from data-hungry deep learning approaches, while transfer of models trained on data-rich neighboring basins suffers from systematic shifts in geology, climate, and surface cover \citep{zhu2026thawsusceptibility}.

Two strategies have emerged to mitigate this. \textbf{Transfer learning} by fine-tuning a model pre-trained on data-rich basins \citep{ghorbanzadeh2022landslide} reduces label requirements but still needs tens to hundreds of target samples. \textbf{Domain adaptation} approaches such as DANN \citep{ganin2015dann} attempt to learn domain-invariant representations from labeled source data and unlabeled target data, with mixed success in remote sensing \citep{tuia2016domain, huang2026transferability}; recent work on source-free domain adaptation for cross-region Sentinel time-series mapping further documents how p(x) and p(y$\mid$x) shifts dominate cross-site transfer \citep{mohammadi2024sourcefreeunsupervised}.

A complementary paradigm, \textbf{meta-learning} \citep{finn2017maml, nichol2018reptile}, trains a model to \emph{adapt rapidly to new tasks from a few examples}. Meta-learning has shown promise in remote sensing for land-cover classification \citep{russwurm2020metalearning}, satellite image segmentation \citep{tseng2022timm}, and cross-scenario hyperspectral-to-Sentinel-2 trait estimation \citep{fu2025crossscenario}, and a recent single-site application \citep{ma2025metalsm} demonstrated temporal meta-learning for landslide susceptibility in Hong Kong. However, to our knowledge, no published study has systematically evaluated meta-learning for \emph{cross-basin} landslide susceptibility transfer, particularly across the extreme hyper-arid-to-humid climate gradient of Chile, which spans $\sim$32$^\circ$ of latitude and where traditional transfer learning is difficult. A second, more basic obstacle cuts across all of these strategies: how transfer is \emph{measured}. Spatially explicit cross-validation, essential for unbiased evaluation on autocorrelated Earth-observation data \citep{brenning2005spatial}, remains under-applied in landslide remote sensing; benchmarks are routinely reported on balanced test sets at a fixed threshold, with episodes or folds pooled as though independent, and with negatives drawn far from the positives. Each of these conventions biases the reported skill upward, and their combined effect on \emph{cross-basin} transfer has not, to our knowledge, been quantified. A transfer result that survives only under permissive validation cannot support the operational claim it is usually made to carry.

In this work we quantify that gap and then ask what survives it. Using landslide susceptibility across a $\sim$32$^\circ$ hydro-climatic gradient in Chile as the test bed, and first-order meta-learning as the transfer paradigm under scrutiny---the first systematic \emph{cross-basin} evaluation of it, complementing the temporal single-site setting of \citet{ma2025metalsm}---we measure how much each evaluation convention inflates apparent transfer, and re-examine the methods under the stricter protocol. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We construct a unified, balanced dataset spanning \textbf{14 Chilean basins} (10 source, 4 target) with a consistent 58-feature cross-basin schema: 30~m MERIT-DEM derivatives (terrain, hydrology, texture, focal statistics), WorldClim bioclimatic variables, and SERNAGEOMIN geology categoricals. Inventory labels span four published catalogs and one ad-hoc digitization (3{,}290 positives + 3{,}290 negatives after balanced sampling).
    \item We benchmark \textbf{five methods}---Independent (no source), Fine-tune (concat-source pretrain), Reptile, FOMAML, DANN---plus \textbf{four classical machine-learning baselines} (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, CatBoost), across $K$-shot evaluation ($K \in \{1, 5, 10, 20\}$) on four target basins, with three random seeds and bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (70{,}200 $K$-shot model fits in total across primary, classical and extended baselines).
    \item We \textbf{quantify how much four standard evaluation conventions inflate apparent cross-basin transfer}, each isolated as a controlled contrast: random versus spatially disjoint validation ($5$--$27$~pp $F_1$), balanced test sets at a fixed threshold versus operational prevalence (precision falls to $0.07$--$0.10$), pooling correlated episodes versus a cluster-aware bootstrap (confidence intervals $3.4\times$ too narrow), and permissive versus slope-matched negative sampling. We further verify that a fixed adaptation budget does not favour the meta-learned models over the from-scratch baseline. Together these define an evaluation protocol for cross-basin transfer that we release with the benchmark.
    \item Our findings: (i) meta-learning (Reptile or FOMAML) wins $K=1$ over Independent in 4/4 target basins under spatial CV, with a mean FOMAML lift of $+5.5$\,pp $F_1$ (range $+4.3$ to $+6.8$\,pp; Reptile mean $+6.2$\,pp, up to $+7.8$\,pp), statistically significant under a cluster-level paired test in three of the four targets; Reptile and FOMAML perform within overlapping confidence intervals in the majority of target-$K$ cells; (ii) the advantage decays monotonically with $K$ and vanishes by $K=20$; (iii) DANN does not improve over Independent on average and degrades substantially in the smallest target basin (Copiap\'o $N=19$: $-7$\,pp at $K=1$), suggesting adversarial alignment is counter-productive when both source domains and target inventories are small; (iv) adaptation curves confirm meta-learned initializations reach $F_1 \approx 0.78$ within $\leq 3$ gradient steps in Copiap\'o versus $>100$ steps for Independent; (v) a pooled-source logistic regression is competitive with meta-learning in peak $F_1$ at every $K$ tested, so the benchmark's central claim is not predictive superiority: it is the mechanistic evidence that a transferable geomorphic prior exists---episodic meta-training extracts it within a neural architecture where naive pooled pretraining (Fine-tune, Meta-Baseline) fails---plus a quantification of the narrow few-shot regime where that prior pays off.
\end{enumerate}
