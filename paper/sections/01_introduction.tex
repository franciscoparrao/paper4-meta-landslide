\revtext{Remote sensing has reshaped landslide susceptibility mapping by delivering globally consistent inputs. These include Sentinel-1 C-band SAR products for surface-deformation and amplitude features \citep{poggi2026sentinellandslide}, Sentinel-2 multi-spectral composites for surface cover and vegetation indices, MERIT and Copernicus 30~m DEMs for topographic and hydrologic derivatives, and WorldClim 2.1 bioclimatic surfaces for environmental context. These data, harmonized to a common grid, feed the dominant paradigm: a machine-learning classifier is fitted to a per-basin inventory of labeled events. This workflow produces strong results when local training data are abundant \citep{reichenbach2018review, merghadi2020ml, ma2025metalsm}, and it sits within the broader trajectory of deep-learning adoption across photogrammetric and remote-sensing tasks documented by \citet{heipke2020deeplearning}. However, the practitioner facing a \emph{newly-studied} basin confronts a data-scarcity bottleneck. Digitizing a usable inventory typically requires expert geomorphological mapping over hundreds of events, an effort that can take months and is rarely funded for individual watersheds. As a result, basins with sparse inventories ($N<50$ mass-movement records) cannot benefit from data-hungry deep learning approaches. At the same time, transfer of models trained on data-rich neighboring basins suffers from systematic shifts in geology, climate, and surface cover \citep{zhu2026thawsusceptibility}.}

\revtext{Whether a model transfers across basins is, however, a question that can only be answered as well as it is measured, and the conventions under which cross-basin transfer is usually measured are permissive. Spatially explicit cross-validation is essential for unbiased evaluation on autocorrelated Earth-observation data \citep{brenning2005spatial, roberts2017cv, ploton2020spatialvalidation}, yet it remains under-applied in landslide remote sensing. Benchmarks are routinely reported on balanced test sets at a fixed threshold, with episodes or folds pooled as though they were independent, and with negatives drawn far from the positives. Features are routinely selected on the pooled data of all study areas, including the ones later used to test transfer. Each of these conventions biases the reported skill upward, and their combined effect on \emph{cross-basin} transfer has not, to our knowledge, been quantified. A transfer result that survives only under permissive validation cannot support the operational claim it is usually made to carry. This paper asks how much the standard conventions overstate cross-basin transfer, and what survives when they are removed.}

\revtext{We use few-shot transfer with meta-learning as the test case because it is the paradigm that promises the most from the least data. Two strategies have been used to mitigate data scarcity in new basins. \textbf{Transfer learning} by fine-tuning a model pre-trained on data-rich basins \citep{ghorbanzadeh2022landslide} reduces label requirements but still needs tens to hundreds of target samples. \textbf{Domain adaptation} approaches such as DANN \citep{ganin2015dann} learn domain-invariant representations from labeled source data and unlabeled target data, with mixed success in remote sensing \citep{tuia2016domain, huang2026transferability}. Source-free adaptation for cross-region Sentinel time-series mapping documents how shifts in $p(x)$ and $p(y\mid x)$ dominate cross-site transfer \citep{mohammadi2024sourcefreeunsupervised}. \textbf{Meta-learning} \citep{finn2017maml, nichol2018reptile} trains a model to \emph{adapt rapidly to new tasks from a few examples}. It has shown promise in remote sensing for land-cover classification \citep{russwurm2020metalearning}, satellite image classification \citep{tseng2022timm} and cross-scenario trait estimation \citep{fu2025crossscenario}, and it has reached landslide susceptibility in two forms. \citet{chen2022metalsm} meta-learned an intermediate representation for few-shot susceptibility prediction across spatial blocks of two large study areas, and \citet{ma2025metalsm} applied temporal meta-learning to dynamic susceptibility at a single site in Hong Kong. Neither evaluated transfer between hydrologically distinct watersheds, under spatially disjoint validation, against domain-adaptation and classical baselines, nor quantified how the evaluation protocol itself shapes the apparent advantage. This is the gap we address.}

\revtext{Our test bed is landslide susceptibility across 14 Chilean watersheds: ten source basins spanning a $\sim$32$^\circ$ hydro-climatic gradient and four semi-arid target basins of north-central Chile. With first-order meta-learning as the transfer paradigm under scrutiny, we measure how much each evaluation convention inflates apparent transfer, and we re-examine the methods under the stricter protocol. Our contributions are:}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \revtext{We \textbf{quantify how much five standard evaluation conventions inflate apparent cross-basin transfer}, each isolated as a controlled contrast on identical data. Random validation, relative to spatially disjoint validation, inflates $F_1$ by up to $+21$~pp in the most heterogeneous basin and not at all in another. Under operational prevalence rather than balanced test sets at a fixed threshold, precision falls to $0.05$--$0.09$. Pooling correlated episodes, rather than using a partition-level bootstrap, gives confidence intervals $3.3\times$ too narrow. Selecting features with the labels of the target basins, rather than with source labels only, doubles the $K=1$ meta-learning advantage from $+2.4$ to $+4.7$~pp under identical seeds (matched-seed control; the main benchmark, with five seeds and its own initializations, gives $+4.3$~pp). Replacing a size-weighted pooled baseline with a basin-balanced one makes the apparent advantage of episodic meta-training over pooled pre-training disappear. We further verify that neither the adaptation budget, the negative-sampling rule, the number of spatial folds, nor exclusion buffers of up to 20~km around the held-out area manufacture the reported advantage. Together, these contrasts and controls define an evaluation protocol for cross-basin transfer that we release with the benchmark.}
    \item \revtext{We construct a unified, balanced dataset spanning \textbf{14 Chilean basins} (10 source, 4 target) with a consistent 58-feature cross-basin schema. The schema comprises 30~m MERIT-DEM derivatives (terrain, hydrology, texture, focal statistics), WorldClim bioclimatic variables, and SERNAGEOMIN geology categoricals, of which source-only feature selection retains nine morphometric descriptors. Inventory labels span four published catalogs and one ad-hoc digitization (4{,}591 positive and 4{,}591 negative pixels after balanced sampling, documented basin by basin).}
    \item \revtext{We benchmark \textbf{five methods}---Independent (no source), Fine-tune (pooled pre-training), Reptile, FOMAML, DANN---together with a basin-balanced pooled baseline, a pooled-then-episodic baseline, three further meta-learning and adaptation baselines (ProtoNet, Meta-Baseline, CDAN) and \textbf{four classical machine-learning baselines} (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, CatBoost). The evaluation is $K$-shot ($K \in \{1, 5, 10, 20\}$) on four target basins, with five seeds, hierarchical-bootstrap intervals, a pre-specified joint hypothesis test and Holm-corrected secondary tests. It comprises 81{,}600 $K$-shot model fits in the core benchmark and 215{,}700 including the controls.}
    \item \revtext{Our findings under the strict protocol are as follows. (i) A meta-learned initialization (Reptile) exceeds training from scratch at $K=1$ by $+4.3$~pp $F_1$ jointly across the four targets ($p<0.001$). Per basin, the gain is $+2.6$ to $+7.4$~pp, significant in three of four after Holm correction. The advantage falls to $+1.6$~pp at $K=5$ and to nothing at $K \geq 10$. An independent replication of the protocol with different initializations gives $+2.2$~pp at $K=1$, and one-hot encoding the single categorical input halves it, so the advantage is best described as $2$--$4$~pp. (ii) This advantage is reproduced by pooled pre-training once source basins are weighted equally: episodic training and basin-balanced pooled training are indistinguishable ($-0.04$~pp), so what transfers is a basin-balanced source representation, not the episodic optimization. (iii) A pooled-source logistic regression matches or exceeds every neural method at $K=1$ in all four targets. (iv) Domain-adversarial alignment does not help at $K=1$ and is weakly positive at $K=5$. (v) The gains in ROC-AUC ($+0.05$ to $+0.14$) and in prevalence-weighted average precision survive re-weighting to a 5\% landslide prevalence, where absolute precision collapses to $0.05$--$0.09$ for every method.}
\end{enumerate}
