We present the first cross-basin application of first-order meta-learning (Reptile, FOMAML) to landslide susceptibility classification across 14 Chilean watersheds spanning an extreme Atacama-to-Patagonia climate gradient, evaluated with rigorous spatial cross-validation and complementary to the temporal single-site setting of \citet{ma2025metalsm}. With 70{,}200 $K$-shot model fits across methods, $K$-shot regimes, three to five seeds, and dual cross-validation protocols, we demonstrate that meta-learning (Reptile or FOMAML) outperforms an Independent baseline at the most extreme few-shot regime ($K=1$) in 4 of 4 target basins under spatial cross-validation, with a mean FOMAML lift of $+5.5$~pp $F_1$ (range $+4.3$ to $+6.8$~pp; Reptile mean $+6.2$~pp, up to $+7.8$~pp). A cluster-level paired test that respects the spatial-fold structure confirms this advantage is statistically significant in three of the four targets (Huasco, Elqui, Limar\'i; Wilcoxon $p \leq 0.03$), and of similar magnitude but not resolvable in the smallest ($N=19$ Copiap\'o, nine spatial partitions). Reptile and FOMAML achieve statistically comparable performance with overlapping bootstrap CIs in most target-$K$ cells. The advantage decays with $K$ and disappears by $K=20$, defining the practical envelope of applicability.

Three findings carry methodological weight beyond the landslide application:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A transferable geomorphic prior exists, and how it is extracted matters}. Within one architecture, episodic meta-training transfers across basins while naive pooled pretraining does not: Fine-tune gains $\pm 1$~pp over Independent and a frozen concat-pre-trained encoder (Meta-Baseline) fails outright, whereas three structurally distinct meta-learners (Reptile, FOMAML, ProtoNet) converge on similar gains. This is the paper's central claim---not predictive superiority, since a pooled-source logistic regression remains competitive in peak $F_1$ at every $K$ we tested. What meta-learning uniquely provides is an initialization that specialises to a new basin in $\leq 3$ gradient steps.
    \item \textbf{Precipitation similarity shows no detectable relation to transfer success}. With four targets and a single-variable proxy the test is underpowered, so this is a preliminary null rather than established climate invariance; it is nonetheless consistent with a prior built on DEM morphometry, and suggests climate matching is not the first-order source-selection criterion.
    \item \textbf{Domain-adversarial alignment does not help, and may hurt with very small targets}. DANN improves over Independent in no target, and its largest point-estimate deficit is in Copiap\'o ($N=19$, $5$--$7$~pp $F_1$), although that basin's nine spatial partitions do not resolve the deficit as significant. The robust statement is the absence of any adversarial advantage under a small source pool ($N=10$).
\end{enumerate}

Limitations include the use of a small tabular MLP backbone (vs.\ U-Net spatial models), static climate features, and a 10-source pool that is small by meta-learning standards. Future work should extend to U-Net spatial architectures, trigger-aware meta-learning to handle intra-basin heterogeneity (e.g., Elqui), and richer source pools spanning Andean countries.

For practitioners, the operational recommendation is deliberately modest: a pooled-source logistic regression is a legitimate default for basin-scale susceptibility screening, and the meta-learned initialization earns its added complexity in the narrow regime this benchmark delimits---one or two verified events, where a specialised model must be produced in minutes rather than after an inventory campaign. The released benchmark, datasets and evaluation protocol are intended to make that regime testable elsewhere in Latin America, where data-scarce basins outnumber data-rich ones and where rigorous spatial validation remains the exception rather than the rule.
