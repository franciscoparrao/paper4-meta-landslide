\subsection{Spatial-CV $K$-shot benchmark}
\label{sec:results:spatial}

\revtext{Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results} and Figure~\ref{fig:lift} give the main result under spatially disjoint validation with the source-only feature set and five seeds. At $K=1$, the meta-learned Reptile initialization has the highest mean $F_1$ in all four target basins (Copiap\'o $0.594$, Huasco $0.711$, Elqui $0.470$, Limar\'i $0.474$, against Independent $0.519$, $0.685$, $0.434$ and $0.436$), with FOMAML second in three. The paired cluster-level differences to Independent (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_paired}) are $+7.4$~pp in Copiap\'o, $+2.6$ in Huasco, $+3.6$ in Elqui and $+3.8$ in Limar\'i for Reptile, and $+2.0$ to $+3.5$~pp for FOMAML. Fine-tune, the size-weighted pooled pre-training, is within $\pm 2$~pp of Independent in every basin ($+2.2$, $+0.1$, $+0.6$, $-0.6$), and DANN is neutral or negative ($+2.8$, $-2.5$, $-0.2$, $+1.3$). The advantage decays with the support size: averaged over targets, Reptile exceeds Independent by $+4.3$~pp at $K=1$, $+1.6$~pp at $K=5$, $-0.4$~pp at $K=10$ and $+0.6$~pp at $K=20$. The few-shot regime in which a source-learned initialization pays off therefore ends at about ten labeled events per class. Absolute discrimination remains modest in the two heterogeneous targets, Elqui and Limar\'i, where no neural method exceeds $F_1 \approx 0.50$ at $K=1$ or $0.65$ at $K=20$.}

\revtext{The hierarchical intervals of Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results} are wide, and those of Reptile and Independent overlap in every cell. In Copiap\'o, whose 15 valid partitions each hold 7--12 pixels, the intervals span $\pm 0.1$. This is expected: marginal intervals ignore the pairing on shared partitions and are not the appropriate significance criterion; the paired tests of the next section are.}

\subsection{\revtext{Inference: joint test, per-basin tests and the replication unit}}
\label{sec:results:inference}

\revtext{The pre-specified primary hypothesis---Reptile exceeds Independent at $K=1$ jointly across the four targets---is confirmed. The joint stratified permutation test gives a mean paired difference of $+4.3$~pp (95\% hierarchical-bootstrap CI $+2.9$ to $+5.7$~pp) with $p<0.001$ over 72 partitions (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_paired}, joint row). FOMAML gives $+2.8$~pp ($p<0.001$), whereas Fine-tune ($+0.6$~pp, $p=0.43$) and DANN ($+0.3$~pp, $p=0.73$) do not differ from Independent. At $K=5$ the joint Reptile difference is $+1.6$~pp ($p=0.01$), and at $K=10$ and $K=20$ it is not distinguishable from zero.}

\revtext{The secondary, per-basin tests are weaker, as they must be with 10--25 partitions per basin. Before correction, Reptile is significant in Copiap\'o ($p=0.002$), Elqui ($p=0.005$) and Limar\'i ($p=0.007$) but not in Huasco ($p=0.13$). After Holm correction over the eight secondary tests, Copiap\'o ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.014$), Elqui ($0.028$) and Limar\'i ($0.037$) remain significant and Huasco does not ($0.39$). Huasco is the basin in which the five seeds produce identical K-means partitions (adjusted Rand index 1.00; Table~\ref{tab:fold_diagnostics}), so its ten $(\text{seed},\text{fold})$ partitions are two spatial folds evaluated five times. The test there has effectively two spatial replicates and cannot resolve a $+2.6$~pp effect. Under the most conservative replication unit, the fold with seeds averaged ($n=5$ or fewer per basin), no per-basin test can reach $p<0.05$ by construction: the smallest attainable Wilcoxon $p$ is $0.0625$. The sign is nevertheless positive in 5/5 folds in Copiap\'o, Elqui and Limar\'i and in 3/4 in Huasco, and the joint test at this unit remains significant ($p<0.001$).}

\revtext{Two further observations qualify the size of the effect. First, pooling episodes as if independent would have given confidence intervals a median $3.3\times$ narrower than the hierarchical ones (Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results} reports the latter). Second, an independent replication of the whole protocol used the same seeds but different network initializations (the $2\times2$ experiment of Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting}, which re-trains Independent and Reptile). It gives a joint $K=1$ difference of $+2.2$~pp rather than $+4.3$~pp, with the same ordering of basins. The variance between runs of the meta-training procedure is therefore of the same order as the effect. It is not captured by the partition-level intervals, which condition on the trained meta-models. This is the reason we describe the $K=1$ advantage as $2$--$4$~pp rather than as a point estimate.}

\subsection{\revtext{Feature selection with target labels inflates the transfer advantage}}
\label{sec:results:leakage}

\revtext{The submitted version of this benchmark selected features on all 14 basins and reported a $K=1$ advantage of $+5.5$ to $+6.2$~pp. To isolate the effect of that leakage from every other change, we re-ran the $2\times2$ design of Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting} twice with identical seeds, partitions and support draws. One run used the eleven features selected using target labels, and the other used the nine selected on source labels only (Table~\ref{tab:cfs_comparison}). The mean $K=1$ Reptile advantage over Independent is $+4.7$~pp with the leaky set and $+2.4$~pp with the clean set. Per basin, the change is $+5.0 \to +2.6$ (Copiap\'o), $+3.3 \to +1.5$ (Huasco), $+2.5 \to +3.2$ (Elqui) and $+8.0 \to +2.1$~pp (Limar\'i). At $K \geq 5$ the two feature sets give the same advantage ($1.4$ vs.\ $1.5$~pp at $K=5$; $0.4$ vs.\ $0.4$ at $K=10$). Absolute $F_1$ at $K=1$ falls by 1--10~pp without the two leaked features, because annual precipitation and geomorphons carry genuine within-basin signal in the targets. The leaky selection also made episodic training look superior to a basin-balanced pooled baseline ($+1.7$, $+1.5$, $-1.6$ and $+1.9$~pp in the four targets), a difference that vanishes with the clean set (next section). Among the extended baselines, ProtoNet lost the most ($-9.8$ and $-9.5$~pp in Copiap\'o and Huasco), and pooled logistic regression the least ($\leq 3$~pp). Selecting features with the labels of the basins later used to test transfer is thus a fifth convention that overstates few-shot transfer: here it doubled the headline advantage.}

\subsection{\revtext{Domain weighting versus episodic training}}
\label{sec:results:weighting}

\revtext{Episodic meta-training samples source basins uniformly, whereas the concatenated pre-training of Fine-tune weights each basin by its sample count, so that Taltal supplies 49\% of the pre-training positives. The two baselines therefore differ in domain weighting as well as in optimization. Table~\ref{tab:pooled_vs_episodic} evaluates the $2\times2$ design \{size-weighted, basin-balanced\}~$\times$~\{pooled, episodic\} on identical partitions and support draws with five seeds. Weighting the source basins equally in pooled pre-training raises $F_1$ at $K=1$ by $+1.6$~pp on average ($+1.7$, $+0.7$, $+1.1$ and $+2.6$~pp; significant in Elqui and Limar\'i). Given balanced weighting, episodic training adds nothing: Reptile minus Fine-tune (balanced) is $-1.4$, $+0.5$, $+0.6$ and $+0.2$~pp ($-0.04$~pp on average, no basin significant). Initializing Reptile from the pooled pre-trained weights (Pretrain$\rightarrow$Reptile) equals Reptile from scratch ($-0.1$~pp) and exceeds size-weighted Fine-tune by $+1.5$~pp, significant in three basins. At $K=5$ the pattern is the same at half the magnitude, and at $K \geq 10$ all contrasts are within $\pm 0.7$~pp.}

\revtext{The interpretation is direct. What transfers from the source pool to a new basin is a representation in which every source basin has had equal influence. Episodic optimization is one way to obtain it and basin-balanced pooled pre-training is another, and the two are indistinguishable under spatially disjoint, leakage-free evaluation. The failure of Fine-tune in the submitted version was a failure of size-weighted pooling, not of pooled pre-training as such. This also explains the behaviour of Meta-Baseline, whose frozen encoder is size-weighted (Section~\ref{sec:results:extended}).}

\subsection{\revtext{Random versus spatial cross-validation}}
\label{sec:results:random}

\revtext{In random cross-validation, support and query pixels are drawn without regard to location (Figure~\ref{fig:f1_random}). This protocol overstates spatial-CV $F_1$ by 13--21~pp in Elqui and by 5--25~pp in Limar\'i at every $K$, and by 7--9~pp at $K=5$ in Copiap\'o (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_vs_random}). It does not do so uniformly: in Copiap\'o at $K=1$ the two protocols agree within 2~pp. In Huasco at $K=1$, spatial CV is $6$~pp \emph{higher} than random CV, and the two converge only at $K=20$. The Huasco inversion follows from the fold structure documented in Section~\ref{sec:results:spatialindep}: the spatial folds that remain valid are the event-dense mountainous clusters. The random query, by contrast, mixes the whole sampled basin, including low-relief pixels that are harder to classify from morphometry alone. The inflation by random CV is therefore real and large in the heterogeneous basins. It is, however, a property of each basin's spatial structure rather than a constant to be subtracted, which is one more reason to report spatial CV directly. The relative ranking of methods is preserved under both protocols, and under random CV the $K=1$ Reptile advantage is $+7.3$, $+4.5$, $+3.3$ and $+1.9$~pp in Copiap\'o, Huasco, Elqui and Limar\'i.}

\subsection{\revtext{Spatial independence of the folds}}
\label{sec:results:spatialindep}
\label{sec:results:variogram}

\revtext{Spatially disjoint folds do not by themselves guarantee that query pixels are far from support pixels. We measured the separation directly (Table~\ref{tab:fold_diagnostics}): over the valid $K=1$ partitions, the distance from a held-out pixel to the nearest support pixel is never below $1.9$~km (Huasco), $2.2$~km (Elqui), $3.9$~km (Limar\'i) or $16.6$~km (Copiap\'o). No held-out pixel lies within 1~km of the support, and the median separation is 17--44~km. Residual variograms of the Independent model, fitted with a spherical model, give autocorrelation ranges of 30~km (Copiap\'o), 45~km (Huasco), 21~km (Elqui) and 17~km (Limar\'i) (Figure~\ref{fig:variogram}). The median separation exceeds the range in Copiap\'o and Limar\'i, is comparable to it in Elqui, and is below it in Huasco.}

\revtext{Because distance summaries are indirect, we repeated the full $K$-shot benchmark removing all support pixels within 2, 5, 10 and 20~km of the held-out cluster (Table~\ref{tab:buffered_cv}, Figure~\ref{fig:buffered}). Buffers up to 10~km remove at most 3.5\% of the support pool and leave the joint $K=1$ Reptile advantage unchanged ($+4.4$, $+4.2$ and $+4.3$~pp, all $p<0.001$). The 20~km buffer, of the order of the variogram ranges, removes 14\% of the support in Huasco and Limar\'i and 21\% in Elqui, and halves the number of valid Huasco partitions. It attenuates the joint advantage to $+3.6$~pp ($p<0.001$; $+6.3$, $+0.8$, $+4.6$ and $+2.5$~pp per basin). Unbuffered spatial CV therefore overstates the few-shot advantage by less than 1~pp relative to a buffer comparable to the autocorrelation range.}

\revtext{Two further properties of the partitions matter for interpretation. First, the K-means partitions of different seeds overlap strongly (adjusted Rand index 0.93 in Copiap\'o, 1.00 in Huasco, 0.84 in Elqui, 0.70 in Limar\'i). This is why inference is reported at the partition level and with the fold as a sensitivity unit (Section~\ref{sec:results:inference}). Second, the excluded partitions are not random: every exclusion is a held-out cluster containing a single class (Copiap\'o 10/25, Huasco 15/25, Limar\'i 3/25, Elqui 0/25). In Huasco and Limar\'i, the excluded clusters are the flat, event-free parts of the basin, mapped in Figure~\ref{fig:fold_partitions} (slope standard deviation 1.7 vs.\ 6.7 in the retained clusters of Huasco; MRVBF 2.4 vs.\ 0.8; Kolmogorov--Smirnov $p<0.001$ for eight of nine features). The benchmark thus characterizes transfer within the event-bearing mountainous terrain of each basin, where $F_1$ is defined, not across the entire catchment. Finally, re-running the benchmark with 5, 10 and 20 spatial folds (Figure~\ref{fig:kfolds}) shows that the $K=1$ Reptile advantage persists at every granularity in Copiap\'o, Elqui and Huasco. In Limar\'i it is $+3.7$~pp with 5 folds but $-0.5$ and $+0.7$~pp with 10 and 20 folds, where held-out clusters contain very few events. We therefore do not claim a fold-independent advantage in Limar\'i.}

\subsection{Prevalence-robust evaluation}
\label{sec:results:prevalence}

\revtext{The $F_1$ of the main benchmark is computed at threshold $0.5$ on held-out clusters whose positive share (3--86\%) is far above the prevalence of landslide pixels in a basin. As a result, it does not reflect the extreme class imbalance of operational mapping.} We therefore assess the $K=1$ advantage with two prevalence-aware lenses (Figure~\ref{fig:pr_prevalence}). \revtext{First, the \textbf{ROC-AUC} is threshold- and prevalence-independent: under spatial CV at $K=1$, Reptile exceeds Independent in all four targets (Copiap\'o $0.684$ vs.\ $0.544$, Huasco $0.671$ vs.\ $0.621$, Elqui $0.693$ vs.\ $0.606$, Limar\'i $0.577$ vs.\ $0.497$; $+0.05$ to $+0.14$). The discrimination gain is thus not an artifact of the operating point. Second, we translate the operating characteristics to realistic prevalence via $\text{precision}(\pi) = \pi\,\text{TPR} / [\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}]$ and recompute the precision--recall curve \emph{conditional on an assumed} prevalence $\pi = 0.05$, a plausible fraction of hazard-prone terrain. The raw pixel-level inventory prevalence, $10^{-4}$--$10^{-3}$ in these basins, is far lower, and the released result file \texttt{results/stats/prevalence\_robust.csv} reports precision at $\pi \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10\}$. Unlike ROC-AUC, average precision depends on $\pi$, and all AP values below are to be read at $\pi=0.05$. At this prevalence, \emph{absolute precision collapses for every method}: precision at threshold $0.5$ is $0.05$--$0.09$, i.e.\ most flagged pixels are false positives. This is a direct, honest consequence of rare-event detection that the fixed-threshold $F_1$ masks. The meta-learning advantage survives the re-weighting: average precision at $\pi=0.05$ favours Reptile over Independent in all four targets (Copiap\'o $0.097$ vs.\ $0.050$; Huasco $0.084$ vs.\ $0.067$; Elqui $0.092$ vs.\ $0.066$; Limar\'i $0.054$ vs.\ $0.045$). The operational reading is twofold. The framework is a terrain-\emph{stratification} tool, whose value is in ranking (captured by AUC and AP) rather than in per-pixel classification. The source-learned initialization's benefit is robust to prevalence, not an artifact of the evaluation operating point.}

\subsection{Susceptibility maps: what the $K=1$ lift looks like on the ground}
\label{sec:results:maps}

\revtext{To translate the benchmark advantage into the product a basin manager would actually use, we apply the $K=1$-adapted models to the \emph{full} $30$\,m grid of two targets. These are Copiap\'o (largest AUC/AP gain, Section~\ref{sec:results:prevalence}; $N=19$) and Elqui (a hard, low-signal basin). We map the mean adapted-model output across the three seeds and twenty support draws (Figure~\ref{fig:maps}).} Maps are shown as within-basin percentiles, the standard susceptibility-class presentation, because the raw $K=1$ outputs are uncalibrated (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limits}). \revtext{In Copiap\'o, both models produce spatially coherent surfaces that track the drainage network and steep valley flanks, and the $19$ inventory events fall predominantly in the upper susceptibility percentiles. The meta-learned (Reptile) surface sharpens the ridge--valley contrast and pulls the western headwater tributaries, where several inventory points cluster, into higher percentiles than the Independent baseline, consistent with its $+7.4$\,pp $F_1$ edge. In Elqui, the maps make the low $F_1 \approx 0.47$ tangible: susceptibility is diffuse and the strong west--east orographic gradient dominates the pattern. Inventory events are scattered across percentile classes rather than concentrated in a coherent high-susceptibility zone. The map view thus corroborates both the headline result and its limit. The source-learned initialization yields a usable first-pass susceptibility layer from a single seed event; in heterogeneous basins, however, the layer is a screening product, not a planning-grade map.}

\subsection{Domain adaptation: DANN and CDAN}
\label{sec:results:dann}

\revtext{DANN does not improve over Independent at $K=1$ (joint $+0.3$~pp, $p=0.73$; Table~\ref{tab:cluster_paired}) and is the only method with a negative paired difference whose interval excludes zero in one basin (Huasco, $-2.5$~pp). At $K=5$ it is weakly positive (joint $+1.7$~pp, $p=0.02$), comparable to Reptile at that $K$, and at $K \geq 10$ it is neutral. The pattern is thus not the uniform negative transfer reported in the submitted version, which was concentrated in Copiap\'o under the leaky feature set. It is an absence of any few-shot advantage: adversarial alignment yields nothing at the support sizes where a source-learned initialization matters most.} To verify this is not an artifact of DANN's decade-old design, we additionally evaluate \textbf{CDAN} \citep{long2018cdan}, a stronger conditional-adversarial method (Table~\ref{tab:extended_baselines}). \revtext{CDAN behaves like DANN: at $K=1$ it reaches $0.540$ (Copiap\'o), $0.669$ (Huasco), $0.452$ (Elqui) and $0.456$ (Limar\'i), below Reptile in every target and within $\pm 2$~pp of Independent in three.} That a modern conditional-adversarial method reproduces the pattern indicates the limitation is intrinsic to adversarial domain alignment under scarce source domains, not specific to the original DANN formulation. \revtext{We hypothesize that adversarial alignment requires both a moderately-sized source pool \emph{and} sufficient target signal to break ties between candidate invariances. The conjunction of few source domains ($N=10$) and very small target supports leaves the gradient-reversal mechanism without either.} We return to this in the Discussion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Adaptation curves and the adaptation budget}
\label{sec:results:adaptation}

\revtext{The meta-learned initializations are discriminative before any adaptation: in Copiap\'o at $K=5$ (random CV, Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation}), FOMAML and Reptile start at $F_1 = 0.63$ and $0.62$ with zero gradient steps, against $0.41$ for a random initialization. They reach $0.70$ and $0.68$ after a single step and plateau there, and Fine-tune reaches $0.66$ in three steps. Independent, trained from random initialization, requires $>100$ steps to approach a comparable plateau.} This validates the practical efficiency benefit of a source-learned initialization: a pre-trained model is immediately deployable for new basins, whereas Independent training requires complete model retraining from random initialization.

\revtext{The main benchmark fixes the adaptation budget at 30 inner steps for \emph{every} method. The slower convergence of Independent therefore raises a fairness question: does the fixed budget under-serve the baseline and thereby inflate the reported lift?} A budget-fairness control answers no. \revtext{We re-ran the $K=1$ spatial-CV protocol with inner-step budgets of 30, 100 and 300 for both Independent and Reptile (shared support draws per episode). Independent gains at most $+1.0$~pp $F_1$ from a $10\times$ larger budget (Copiap\'o $0.512 \to 0.506$, Huasco $0.686 \to 0.688$, Elqui $0.426 \to 0.436$, Limar\'i $0.436 \to 0.435$). With a two-sample support, additional steps saturate or over-fit rather than converge further on held-out queries. The $>100$-step plateau above concerns the \emph{trajectory} of training-loss descent, not the attainable query $F_1$. Measured against Independent at its own best budget, the Reptile lift remains $+1.9$ to $+5.7$~pp across the four targets (\texttt{results/stats/baseline\_budget.csv}), so the fixed 30-step budget does not manufacture the meta-learning advantage. Sensitivity to the meta-training hyperparameters (inner steps $\{1, 3, 5, 10\}$, Reptile $\epsilon \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3\}$, $K_{\text{meta}} \in \{5, 10, 20\}$) is at most $1.4$~pp $F_1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}).}
\label{sec:results:ablation}

\subsection{Classical ML under spatial CV}
\label{sec:results:classical}

\revtext{Logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and CatBoost were evaluated under the identical $K$-shot spatial-CV protocol (Figure~\ref{fig:classical}). The strongest classical configuration is logistic regression trained on the pooled source basins plus the $K$ target samples. At $K=1$ it reaches $0.696$ (Copiap\'o), $0.746$ (Huasco), $0.472$ (Elqui) and $0.503$ (Limar\'i), equal to or above Reptile in every target ($+10.3$, $+3.5$, $+0.2$ and $+2.9$~pp). At $K \geq 5$ the picture reverses in three basins: the meta-learned MLP leads pooled logistic regression by 3--14~pp in Elqui, Huasco and Limar\'i, while pooled logistic regression stays ahead in Copiap\'o at every $K$. Gradient-boosted trees trained on the pooled source win most Limar\'i cells. Pooled logistic regression was also the method least affected by the feature-selection leakage ($\leq 3$~pp). This is consistent with a linear model extracting a transferable morphometric signal that the neural methods only match after balancing the source domains. The operational reading is therefore plain: for a new basin with one or a handful of verified events, a pooled-source logistic regression on nine DEM descriptors is at least as good as any meta-learned neural initialization we tested. It is also simpler to train and audit, and needs no meta-training infrastructure. The neural-network comparison isolates a different question: how the source representation should be formed. Its answer (Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting}) is that balanced domain weighting, not episodic optimization, is what matters.}

\subsection{Modern meta-learning baselines}
\label{sec:results:extended}

\revtext{To test whether the few-shot advantage is specific to first-order methods (Reptile, FOMAML) or general to meta-learning, we evaluate two additional baselines under spatial CV with five seeds: ProtoNet \citep{snell2017protonet} and Meta-Baseline \citep{chen2021metabaseline} (Table~\ref{tab:extended_baselines}, Figure~\ref{fig:extended}). ProtoNet, a metric-based meta-learner with \emph{zero} gradient adaptation at test time, matches the gradient-based meta-learners at $K=1$ (Copiap\'o $0.592$, Huasco $0.709$, Elqui $0.441$, Limar\'i $0.485$ against Reptile's $0.594$, $0.711$, $0.470$ and $0.474$). This corroborates the interpretation of \citet{raghu2020rapid} that the gain comes from a transferable representation, not from the adaptation mechanism. Meta-Baseline, whose encoder is pre-trained on the size-weighted concatenation and then frozen, under-performs every other source-trained method at $K=1$ ($0.499$, $0.655$, $0.406$, $0.422$). This is what the domain-weighting result of Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting} predicts for a size-weighted representation that receives no adaptation. Across the four targets, every episodically trained or balanced source-trained method exceeds Independent at $K=1$, whereas the size-weighted ones do not do so consistently (Fine-tune $+2.2$, $+0.1$, $+0.6$ and $-0.6$~pp; Meta-Baseline below Independent in all four).}

\subsection{\revtext{Preprocessing sensitivity}}
\label{sec:results:preproc}

\revtext{Reviewers asked how the scaling and encoding of the inputs affect models that are sensitive to numerical distance. We re-ran the $K=1$ spatial-CV comparison of Reptile and Independent (three seeds, identical partitions and supports) under four preprocessing variants (\texttt{results/stats/preproc\_sensitivity.csv}). With the per-basin, label-free standardization used throughout the paper, the mean Reptile advantage over the four targets is $+3.2$~pp ($+4.6$, $+1.7$, $+2.6$, $+3.6$ in Copiap\'o, Huasco, Elqui, Limar\'i). Estimating the standardization statistics on the source pool only, so that no target pixel contributes to preprocessing, gives $+2.3$~pp ($+2.7$, $+1.3$, $+3.5$, $+1.5$) with the same ordering of methods in every basin. Estimating them from the $2K=2$ support pixels alone is degenerate (the standard deviation of two samples) and removes the advantage ($-1.9$~pp). It is not a usable option at $K=1$, and we report it only for completeness. One-hot encoding the single categorical feature retained by source-only selection (landform, 11 classes; 19 inputs instead of 9) leaves Independent unchanged ($0.533$ vs.\ $0.528$ mean $F_1$) but lowers Reptile ($0.549$ vs.\ $0.560$). The advantage therefore halves to $+1.6$~pp ($+1.2$, $+1.3$, $+2.9$, $+0.7$); integer-coded landform, which orders the classes along an approximate flat-to-steep gradient, evidently helps the meta-learned representation more than a nominal encoding does. The direction of every comparison is preserved under all three defensible variants. The magnitude of the few-shot advantage, however, depends on these choices by up to a factor of two, which we fold into the $2$--$4$~pp range given in Section~\ref{sec:results:inference}.}

\subsection{Spatial CNN backbone: spatial context substantially improves accuracy}
\label{sec:results:cnn}

\revtext{To quantify how much the point-wise feature representation costs in predictive accuracy, we re-run the four primary methods (Independent, Fine-tune, Reptile, FOMAML) with a small spatial CNN backbone. The backbone consumes $32 \times 32$ patches over the 25 gridded feature channels (Section~\ref{sec:methods:methods}), and the experiment is run on the largest target basin, Huasco (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn}). This experiment uses the gridded rasters directly, not the CFS-selected features, and is therefore unchanged by the feature-selection correction.} \textbf{The CNN backbone outperforms the MLP by 6--13~pp $F_1$ across all methods and $K$ values}. At $K=1$, Independent CNN reaches $F_1 = 0.78$, already exceeding the MLP's best; FOMAML-CNN reaches $0.82$. At $K=20$, all CNN variants reach $F_1 \geq 0.93$ compared to $\sim 0.80$ for the MLP. \revtext{The meta-learning lift over Independent is smaller in the CNN regime ($+4.2$~pp at $K=1$ for FOMAML-CNN, with the two intervals overlapping). This indicates that spatial context absorbs part of the prediction signal that the source-learned initialization captures in the point-wise regime.} Two implications follow, and one competing interpretation must be stated. \revtext{First, the main-benchmark MLP results report a \emph{conservative lower bound} on achievable cross-basin transfer accuracy. The few-shot advantage remains valid as a methodological finding but is not the operational ceiling.} Second, the framework is mechanically applicable to a spatial CNN backbone, and the ordering over Independent is preserved in that regime. \revtext{However, the shrinking lift also admits an alternative reading: \emph{the advantage is largest precisely where the backbone is weakest}, i.e., a stronger spatial representation absorbs the transferable signal the initialization would otherwise contribute. A single-basin demonstration cannot distinguish these interpretations; a multi-target CNN/U-Net meta-learning evaluation is required to resolve them and is left for follow-up work. Until then, the Huasco experiment should be read as a proof of mechanism, not as evidence that the $K$-shot advantage carries unchanged into stronger architectures.}

\subsection{\revtext{Precipitation similarity and transfer}}
\label{sec:results:climate}

\revtext{Source--target similarity in annual precipitation is measured as $|\text{bio\_12}_{\text{source}} - \text{bio\_12}_{\text{target}}|$, computed from the full feature table since bio\_12 is no longer a selected feature. Whether this similarity predicts the FOMAML advantage at $K=5$ cannot be resolved with four targets: Pearson $r = -0.55$ ($p=0.45$), Spearman $\rho = -0.60$ ($p=0.40$) (Figure~\ref{fig:climate}). We report it as an underpowered null from a single-variable proxy and draw no design conclusion from it (Section~\ref{sec:disc:climate}).}
