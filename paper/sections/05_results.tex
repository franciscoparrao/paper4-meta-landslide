\subsection{Random-CV $K$-shot benchmark}
\label{sec:results:random}

Under random cross-validation (Figure~\ref{fig:f1_random}), meta-learning wins $K=1$ in all four target basins, with best-meta-learner advantages of $+5.4$ to $+8.8$ percentage points (pp) $F_1$ over Independent: FOMAML leads in Huasco, Reptile leads in Elqui and Limar\'i, and the two are tied in Copiap\'o ($F_1 = 0.675$ both). The advantage narrows to 0--2~pp at $K=10$ and disappears at $K=20$, where all methods converge to within 1~pp $F_1$. This pattern is consistent with the theoretical expectation that meta-learning's advantage scales inversely with available task-specific data.

\subsection{Spatial-CV $K$-shot benchmark}
\label{sec:results:spatial}

Under spatial cross-validation---where support and query are sampled from disjoint K-means clusters of the basin---\textbf{meta-learning (Reptile or FOMAML) wins $K=1$ in 4/4 target basins} (Figure~\ref{fig:lift}, Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results}). Reptile achieves the nominally highest $F_1$ at $K=1$ in all four targets (Copiap\'o $0.665$, Huasco $0.755$, Elqui $0.476$, Limar\'i $0.528$), with FOMAML statistically tied in each (Copiap\'o $0.663$); the inter-method gap between Reptile and FOMAML is within bootstrap standard error in every case. FOMAML achieves $+5.5$~pp $F_1$ mean advantage over Independent across targets at $K=1$ (range $+4.3$ to $+6.8$~pp; Reptile mean $+6.2$~pp, up to $+7.8$~pp in Huasco), decaying monotonically to $-1.1$~pp at $K=20$ (over the three basins where $K=20$ is feasible); Reptile retains $+1.9$~pp at $K=20$, within CI overlap of Independent. Fine-tune provides essentially no benefit (within $\pm 1$~pp).

We assess the significance of the $K=1$ advantage with the cluster-level paired test described in Section~\ref{sec:methods:protocol} (Table~\ref{tab:cluster_paired}), which respects the fact that episodes within a spatial partition are not independent. The 95\% hierarchical-bootstrap CI of the per-cluster paired difference (meta-learning $-$ Independent) \textbf{excludes zero in three of the four targets}: Huasco (Reptile $+7.8$~pp [$+4.8$, $+11.6$], all 6/6 clusters positive, Wilcoxon $p=0.03$), Elqui ($+4.7$~pp [$+2.6$, $+6.9$], 13/15, $p=0.001$) and Limar\'i ($+6.7$~pp [$+3.4$, $+9.8$], 10/12, $p=0.007$). In Copiap\'o the advantage is of similar magnitude but \emph{not} statistically resolved ($+5.7$~pp [$-0.1$, $+12.9$], 7/9 clusters positive, $p=0.16$): its 19-event inventory yields only nine $(\text{seed}, \text{fold})$ clusters, too few to separate a $\sim6$~pp effect from noise. Neither Fine-tune nor DANN is significant in any target. The naive flat bootstrap that pools episodes as independent understates CI width by a median factor of $3.4\times$ ($6.4$ vs.\ $20.7$~pp), and its marginal per-method CIs should not be read as a significance test.

The spatial CV protocol yields lower absolute $F_1$ magnitudes than random CV (drop of 1~pp in Huasco to 27~pp in Elqui; Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_vs_random}), confirming that random CV overestimates absolute performance due to spatial autocorrelation, but \emph{the relative ranking of methods is preserved}.

\subsection{Prevalence-robust evaluation}
\label{sec:results:prevalence}

Because the balanced $F_1$ of the main benchmark is computed on 1:1 queries at threshold $0.5$, it does not reflect the extreme class imbalance of operational mapping. We therefore assess the $K=1$ advantage with two prevalence-aware lenses (Figure~\ref{fig:pr_prevalence}). First, the \textbf{ROC-AUC}, which is threshold- and prevalence-independent: under spatial CV at $K=1$, Reptile exceeds Independent in all four targets---Copiap\'o $0.805$ vs.\ $0.638$, Huasco $0.780$ vs.\ $0.668$, Elqui $0.745$ vs.\ $0.649$, Limar\'i $0.559$ vs.\ $0.527$ (Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results} reports the corresponding $F_1$)---so the discrimination gain is not an artifact of the balanced operating point. Second, we translate the balanced operating characteristics to realistic prevalence via $\text{precision}(\pi) = \pi\,\text{TPR} / [\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1-\pi)\,\text{FPR}]$ and recompute the precision--recall curve at $\pi = 0.05$ (a plausible fraction of hazard-prone terrain; the raw pixel-level inventory prevalence is far lower). At this prevalence \emph{absolute precision collapses for every method}---at $\pi=0.05$ the precision at threshold $0.5$ is only $0.087$ (Reptile) vs.\ $0.069$ (Independent) in Huasco, i.e.\ most flagged pixels are false positives---a direct, honest consequence of rare-event detection that the balanced $F_1$ masks. Crucially, however, \textbf{the meta-learning advantage survives the re-weighting and is proportionally larger}: the average precision at $\pi=0.05$ favours Reptile over Independent in all four targets (Copiap\'o $0.130$ vs.\ $0.066$, a near-doubling; Huasco $0.108$ vs.\ $0.087$; Elqui $0.102$ vs.\ $0.084$; Limar\'i $0.060$ vs.\ $0.048$). The operational reading is twofold: the framework is a terrain-\emph{stratification} tool (its value is in ranking, captured by AUC/AP, not in per-pixel classification), and the meta-learned prior's benefit is robust to prevalence, not an artifact of balanced evaluation.

\subsection{Susceptibility maps: what the $K=1$ lift looks like on the ground}
\label{sec:results:maps}

To translate the benchmark advantage into the product a basin manager would actually use, we apply the $K=1$-adapted models to the \emph{full} $30$\,m grid of two targets---Copiap\'o (largest AUC/AP gain, Section~\ref{sec:results:prevalence}; $N=19$) and Elqui (a hard, low-signal basin)---and map the mean adapted-model output across the three seeds and twenty support draws (Figure~\ref{fig:maps}). Maps are shown as within-basin percentiles, the standard susceptibility-class presentation, because the raw $K=1$ outputs are uncalibrated (Section~\ref{sec:disc:limits}). In Copiap\'o, both models produce spatially coherent surfaces that track the drainage network and steep valley flanks, and the $19$ inventory events fall predominantly in the upper susceptibility percentiles; the meta-learned (Reptile) surface sharpens the ridge--valley contrast and pulls the western headwater tributaries---where several inventory points cluster---into higher percentiles than the Independent baseline, consistent with its $+5.7$\,pp $F_1$ edge. In Elqui, the maps make the low $F_1 \approx 0.48$ tangible: susceptibility is diffuse, the strong west--east orographic gradient dominates the pattern, and inventory events are scattered across percentile classes rather than concentrated in a coherent high-susceptibility zone. The map view thus corroborates both the headline result (the meta-learned prior yields a usable first-pass susceptibility layer from a single seed event) and its honest limit (in heterogeneous basins the layer is a screening product, not a planning-grade map).

\subsection{Domain adaptation: DANN and CDAN both under-perform}
\label{sec:results:dann}

DANN does not improve over Independent on average and shows a strongly basin-dependent pattern (Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results}). The under-performance is concentrated in Copiap\'o, the smallest-inventory target ($N=19$ events), where DANN trails Independent by 5--7~pp $F_1$ across all $K$ values. In the other three targets, DANN is roughly neutral or marginally positive: Huasco $\pm 1$~pp, Elqui $+0.8$ to $+1.6$~pp, Limar\'i $-0.2$ to $+3.2$~pp; at $K=20$, DANN even wins Limar\'i ($0.598$, best of row).

To verify this is not an artifact of DANN's decade-old design, we additionally evaluate \textbf{CDAN} \citep{long2018cdan}, a stronger conditional-adversarial method (Table~\ref{tab:extended_baselines}). CDAN exhibits the same failure mode: in Copiap\'o it reaches only $F_1 = 0.573$ at $K=1$ ($-3.6$~pp vs.\ Independent's $0.609$), and across all targets it remains below the meta-learning methods at $K=1$ (Copiap\'o $0.573$, Huasco $0.707$, Elqui $0.440$, Limar\'i $0.433$). That a modern conditional-adversarial method reproduces the negative-transfer pattern indicates the problem is intrinsic to adversarial domain alignment under scarce source domains, not specific to the original DANN formulation. We hypothesize that adversarial alignment requires both a moderately-sized source pool \emph{and} sufficient target signal to break ties between candidate invariances; the conjunction of few source domains ($N=10$) and a very small target inventory makes the gradient-reversal mechanism susceptible to negative transfer. We return to this in the Discussion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Adaptation curves confirm rapid convergence}
\label{sec:results:adaptation}

FOMAML's meta-learned initialization achieves $F_1 \approx 0.78$ within $\leq 3$ gradient steps in Copiap\'o (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptation}), confirming that the prior is already discriminative and the few-shot inner adaptation is rapid. Reptile reaches its peak in 3--5 steps, Fine-tune in $\sim 10$ steps. Independent, trained from random initialization in the adaptation-curve experiment, requires $>100$ steps before reaching a comparable plateau (well above the 30-step inner budget used in the main benchmark, Section~\ref{sec:methods:protocol}). This validates the practical efficiency benefit of meta-learning: a pre-trained meta-model is immediately deployable for new basins, whereas Independent training requires complete model retraining from random initialization.

Because the main benchmark fixes the adaptation budget at 30 inner steps for \emph{every} method, the slower convergence of Independent raises a fairness question: does the fixed budget under-serve the baseline and thereby inflate the reported lift? A budget-fairness control answers no. Re-running the $K=1$ spatial-CV protocol with inner-step budgets of 30, 100 and 300 for both Independent and Reptile (shared support draws per episode), Independent gains at most $+0.4$~pp $F_1$ from a $10\times$ larger budget (Copiap\'o $0.598 \to 0.601$, Huasco $0.729 \to 0.730$, Elqui $0.441 \to 0.441$, Limar\'i $0.452 \to 0.456$): with a two-sample support, additional steps saturate or over-fit rather than converge further on held-out queries---the $>100$-step plateau above concerns the \emph{trajectory} of training-loss descent, not the attainable query $F_1$. Measured against Independent at its own best budget, the Reptile lift remains $+2.0$ to $+6.7$~pp across the four targets (\texttt{results/stats/baseline\_budget.csv}), so the fixed 30-step budget does not manufacture the meta-learning advantage.

\subsection{Hyperparameter sensitivity}
\label{sec:results:ablation}

Sensitivity to inner steps $\{1, 3, 5, 10\}$, Reptile $\epsilon \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3\}$, and $K_{\text{meta}} \in \{5, 10, 20\}$ is small (within 0.5~pp; Figure~\ref{fig:ablations}). The default configuration (inner steps = 5, $\epsilon = 0.1$, $K_{\text{meta}} = 10$) is robust.

\subsection{No detectable relation between precipitation similarity and transfer success}
\label{sec:results:climate}

We tested whether source-target climate similarity (measured as $|$bio\_12$_{\text{source}} -$ bio\_12$_{\text{target}}|$) predicts FOMAML transfer advantage at $K=5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:climate}). The correlation is negligible (Pearson $r = 0.01$, $p = 0.99$; Spearman $\rho = 0.20$, $p = 0.80$). Two qualifications keep this result in its place: with only four targets the test is severely underpowered, and $|\Delta\text{bio\_12}|$ is a single-variable proxy (annual precipitation), not a full climatic or lithological distance; the weak positive Spearman trend cannot be excluded. What the data do support is the narrower statement that \emph{transfer success is not dominated by precipitation similarity at this scale}---a preliminary indication, not established climate invariance, that climate-matched source selection is not the first-order design concern.

\subsection{Classical ML is competitive under spatial CV}
\label{sec:results:classical}

Logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and CatBoost achieve competitive $F_1$ under spatial CV (Figure~\ref{fig:classical}). Logistic regression wins 7/16 (target, $K$) combinations; random forest wins 4/16. Meta-learning (FOMAML or Reptile) wins 5/16, principally at $K=1$ in Copiap\'o and Limar\'i. We state the strongest classical configuration plainly: logistic regression trained on the pooled source basins plus the $K$ target samples (concat-source mode) is competitive with meta-learning \emph{already at $K=1$}---Copiap\'o $0.696$ vs.\ Reptile $0.665$, Huasco $0.774$ vs.\ $0.755$, Elqui $0.473$ vs.\ $0.476$, Limar\'i $0.516$ vs.\ $0.528$---so on peak $F_1$ alone a practitioner has no reason to prefer meta-learning over a pooled logistic regression at any $K$ we tested. What the neural-network family comparison isolates, however, is the \emph{mechanism}: within the same architecture, episodic meta-training transfers where naive pooling does not (Fine-tune gains $\pm 1$~pp over Independent; the frozen concat-pre-trained Meta-Baseline fails outright, Section~\ref{sec:results:extended}), which is precisely the evidence that a transferable geomorphic prior exists and that \emph{how} it is extracted matters. The operational reading is honest: pooled logistic regression is a legitimate, simpler default for basin-scale susceptibility screening; the meta-learned initialization adds value in the deployment regime (an initialization that specialises in $\leq 3$ gradient steps, Section~\ref{sec:results:adaptation}) and as the instrument that demonstrates cross-basin transferability of the prior.

\subsection{Modern meta-learning baselines confirm the advantage is method-robust}
\label{sec:results:extended}

To test whether the few-shot advantage is specific to first-order methods (Reptile, FOMAML) or general to meta-learning, we evaluate two additional baselines under spatial CV with five seeds: ProtoNet \citep{snell2017protonet} and Meta-Baseline \citep{chen2021metabaseline} (Table~\ref{tab:extended_baselines}, Figure~\ref{fig:extended}). \textbf{ProtoNet is competitive with FOMAML and Reptile} at $K=1$: it achieves the highest $F_1$ of \emph{any} method in Copiap\'o ($0.690$ vs.\ Reptile $0.665$) and Huasco ($0.774$ vs.\ Reptile $0.755$), while trailing slightly in Elqui ($0.436$) and Limar\'i ($0.485$). That a metric-based meta-learner with \emph{zero} gradient adaptation matches gradient-based meta-learners corroborates the interpretation of \citet{raghu2020rapid} that the gain comes from a transferable representation, not from the adaptation mechanism. In contrast, \textbf{Meta-Baseline under-performs} all other meta-learners (e.g., Copiap\'o $K=1$ $F_1 = 0.461$), indicating that for tabular landslide features a frozen concat-pre-trained encoder with a cosine head is insufficient---episodic training (ProtoNet) or gradient adaptation (Reptile/FOMAML) is needed. Across all four target basins, at least one meta-learning method beats the Independent baseline at $K=1$, confirming the advantage is robust to the choice of meta-learner.

\subsection{Variogram validation of spatial cross-validation}
\label{sec:results:variogram}

To verify that the K-means spatial folds (Section~\ref{sec:methods:protocol}) achieve genuine spatial separation rather than merely splitting autocorrelated pixels, we estimate the empirical variogram of Independent-model residuals for each target basin and fit a spherical model to extract the autocorrelation range (Figure~\ref{fig:variogram}). The fitted ranges are 39.3~km (Copiap\'o), 58.5~km (Huasco), 20.9~km (Elqui), and 15.6~km (Limar\'i). Comparing these to the median distance between fold centroids at $K_{\text{folds}}=5$ (70.0, 55.7, 43.6, and 54.2~km respectively), the fold separation \emph{exceeds} the autocorrelation range in three of four basins; only Huasco is marginal ($-2.8$~km), reflecting its larger spatial extent and inventory. Re-running the benchmark at $K_{\text{folds}} \in \{5, 10, 20\}$ confirms that the FOMAML advantage over Independent persists across all fold granularities (per-target mean lift of $+0.4$ to $+2.6$~pp, averaged over $K \in \{5, 10\}$ shots and three seeds; Figure~\ref{fig:kfolds}), demonstrating that the reported advantage is not an artifact of the $K_{\text{folds}}=5$ choice. We adopt $K_{\text{folds}}=5$ as the primary protocol because it balances spatial decorrelation against fold-wise sample sufficiency in the smaller basins.

\subsection{Spatial CNN backbone: spatial context substantially improves accuracy}
\label{sec:results:cnn}

To quantify how much the point-wise feature representation costs in predictive accuracy, we re-run the four primary methods (Independent, Fine-tune, Reptile, FOMAML) with a small spatial CNN backbone consuming $32 \times 32$ patches over the 25 gridded feature channels (Section~\ref{sec:methods:methods}) on the largest target basin, Huasco (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn}). \textbf{The CNN backbone outperforms the MLP by 6--11~pp $F_1$ across all methods and $K$ values}. At $K=1$, Independent CNN reaches $F_1 = 0.78$, already exceeding the MLP's best ($F_1 = 0.76$ for Reptile); FOMAML-CNN reaches $0.82$. At $K=20$, all CNN variants reach $F_1 \geq 0.93$ compared to $\sim 0.84$ for the MLP. The meta-learning lift over Independent is smaller in the CNN regime ($+4.2$~pp at $K=1$ for FOMAML-CNN vs.\ $+6.8$~pp for FOMAML-MLP, with the two intervals overlapping), indicating that spatial context absorbs part of the prediction signal that meta-learning's prior captures in the point-wise regime. Two implications follow, and one competing interpretation must be stated. First, the main-benchmark MLP results report a \emph{conservative lower bound} on achievable cross-basin transfer accuracy; the systematic +5.5~pp meta-learning lift remains valid as a methodological finding but is not the operational ceiling. Second, the framework is mechanically applicable to a spatial CNN backbone, and the meta-learning ordering over Independent is preserved in that regime. However, the shrinking lift also admits the alternative reading that \emph{the meta-learning advantage is largest precisely where the backbone is weakest}---i.e., that a stronger spatial representation absorbs the transferable signal the prior would otherwise contribute---and a single-basin demonstration cannot distinguish these interpretations. A multi-target CNN/U-Net meta-learning evaluation is required to resolve them and is left for follow-up work; until then, the Huasco experiment should be read as a proof of mechanism, not as evidence that the K-shot advantage carries unchanged into stronger architectures.
