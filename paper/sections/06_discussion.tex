\subsection{\revtext{What the meta-learners learned, and what they did not}}
\label{sec:disc:fomaml}

\revtext{Reptile, FOMAML and ProtoNet---averaged-displacement, query-gradient and nearest-prototype meta-learners---reach statistically comparable $F_1$ at $K=1$ in every target (Tables~\ref{tab:spatial_results} and~\ref{tab:extended_baselines}), and ProtoNet does so with no gradient adaptation at all. The convergence of three structurally distinct algorithms, consistent with \citet{raghu2020rapid}, indicates that the few-shot advantage is carried by the source representation rather than by any particular adaptation rule. The $2\times2$ experiment of Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting} then shows what property of that representation matters: equal influence of every source basin. Once pooled pre-training is given the same weighting, it is indistinguishable from episodic training; initialising episodic training from pooled weights changes nothing; and the one source-trained method that fails, Meta-Baseline, is the one whose encoder is both size-weighted and frozen. The practical consequence is that a basin-balanced pooled network, which is cheaper and simpler than any meta-learning algorithm, is the appropriate neural baseline for cross-basin susceptibility transfer, and that the customary size-weighted Fine-tune baseline should no longer be used as the reference against which meta-learning is judged. Among the meta-learners, Reptile remains our recommendation on cost grounds: its update rule is cheaper than FOMAML's query-loss pass and it needs no prototype design.}

\subsection{\revtext{Why adversarial alignment does not help here}}
\label{sec:disc:dann}

\revtext{Neither DANN nor CDAN improves over Independent at $K=1$, and DANN is weakly positive only at $K=5$ (Section~\ref{sec:results:dann}). Two mechanisms are consistent with this. First, adversarial invariance needs domain diversity: with ten source basins the domain classifier can exploit coarse descriptors such as basin-mean relief, and the gradient-reversal layer then discards signal that is also predictive of susceptibility, a form of negative transfer. Second, DANN was designed for the unsupervised setting; re-purposing its feature extractor as the starting point for a supervised $K$-shot fine-tune combines two mechanisms that are not jointly optimised. The robust statement is the absence of any adversarial advantage under a small source pool, not a uniform degradation: the strong Copiap\'o deficit reported in the submitted version did not survive the leakage-free feature set.}

\subsection{Spatial heterogeneity in Elqui: a hard transfer case}
\label{sec:disc:elqui}

Among target basins, Elqui exhibits the largest drop in $F_1$ between random and spatial CV protocols (\revtext{13--21~pp at every $K$}; Section~\ref{sec:results:random}). All methods are constrained to $F_1 \approx 0.47$--$0.55$ under spatial CV at any $K$. This indicates that intra-basin heterogeneity, not the cross-basin transfer capability, is the dominant factor in Elqui.

Two hypotheses are consistent with the data. (i) \textbf{Orographic structure}: Elqui has a strong west-east elevation gradient (sea level to $\sim 5{,}000$ m), with the cordillera dominated by Mesozoic plutonic rocks (granitoids), the precordillera by Cenozoic volcanics, and the coast by Quaternary alluvial deposits. Each lithological unit produces distinct landslide styles (debris flows in the alluvium, rotational slides in the granitoids), and our \revtext{nine-feature morphometric} representation may not capture these axes adequately. (ii) \textbf{Trigger heterogeneity}: the SERNAGEOMIN \emph{catastro} for Elqui aggregates events triggered by the 2015 Atacama floods, the 1997 El Ni\~no, and the 1922 Vallenar earthquake, three regimes with distinct preparatory factors. A meta-model trained on Maule 2010 (seismic) and Cha\~naral (rainfall) sources cannot easily recover this trigger-specific structure from the support set alone.

Future work should address Elqui-style heterogeneity by (a) richer spatial features (e.g., multi-temporal Sentinel-1 SAR coherence), (b) trigger-aware meta-learning, or (c) fold-specific stratification. None of these are straightforward extensions, and we report Elqui as a transparent failure mode rather than attempting to mask it.

\subsection{Climate and transfer: a preliminary null}
\label{sec:disc:climate}

\revtext{The absence of a detectable correlation between source--target precipitation distance and transfer advantage (Section~\ref{sec:results:climate}) is a null from an underpowered test (four targets, one climatic variable), not evidence of climate invariance; its sign even changed between the leaky and the clean feature sets. What the leakage-free selection does establish is narrower and firmer: no climate or geology variable is selected on the source pool, and the nine features that transfer are all DEM morphometry (landform, openness, valley- and ridge-flatness, curvedness, relief and slope variability, DEM texture). This is consistent with the interpretation that the source representation encodes generic susceptibility morphology rather than climate-specific signals, and with the loss of absolute $F_1$ when the within-target precipitation feature was removed. Whether climate-matched source selection would help cannot be answered with four semi-arid targets; the released benchmark is designed to make that test possible as more targets are added.}

\subsection{Implications for remote-sensing-driven susceptibility mapping}
\label{sec:disc:rs}

Three findings carry methodological weight specifically for the remote-sensing-based geohazard community. \emph{First}, the source representation that transfers is recovered from an entirely open, globally-accessible feature stack---MERIT DEM derivatives---without proprietary inputs, optical composites or in-situ measurements. This makes the framework directly replicable in any region with global-DEM coverage. The Chilean focus is illustrative, not restrictive. \emph{Second}, \revtext{source-only feature selection (Section~\ref{sec:data:cfs}) retains DEM-derived terrain, relief and texture descriptors only; climate, hydrology and geology descriptors are not selected on the source pool. This has practical consequences: a 30~m DEM of moderate quality (MERIT, Copernicus GLO-30, or AW3D30) is the binding constraint on deployment, not optical-sensor availability.} \emph{Third}, the advantage of a source-learned initialisation is largest at $K=1$, which corresponds to one verified mass-movement footprint visible in high-resolution Sentinel-2 or Planet imagery. A remote-sensing analyst can typically validate a single landslide via visual interpretation in $<10$ minutes, whereas mapping the $K \geq 50$ events required by per-basin training takes weeks. \revtext{The human-in-the-loop cost thus moves from inventory construction to seed-event validation, with two caveats that the present results impose. The first is scope: given a pre-trained source model and a pre-computed feature stack, adapting to a new basin takes minutes of compute, but assembling the DEM-derived stack for a new basin is a separate preprocessing step of hours to a day, and the few-shot advantage over a pooled logistic regression on the same stack is nil. The second is quality: at operational prevalence the per-pixel precision of every method is $0.05$--$0.09$, so the layer produced from one event is a terrain-stratification product for screening and prioritisation, not a planning-grade map, and in heterogeneous basins such as Elqui it inherits the low discrimination of Table~\ref{tab:spatial_results}.} In the Chilean institutional context, such screening layers are consumable by the agencies that motivated this study: SERNAGEOMIN, which maintains the national \emph{catastro de remociones en masa} used here as the label source, and SENAPRED, whose regional risk-management plans require basin-scale susceptibility inputs. A natural extension is to incorporate multi-temporal Sentinel-1 InSAR coherence and amplitude features \citep{poggi2026sentinellandslide, biggs2026volcanomonitor, elliott2026deformation}, \revtext{and InSAR-augmented inventories of the kind built by \citet{chen2023insarinventory},} to capture surface-deformation precursors that static DEM stacks cannot, an avenue actively explored for global InSAR landslide monitoring \citep{guo2026globalmonitor}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limits}

\revtext{We acknowledge ten limitations.}

\paragraph{\revtext{Geographic coverage of the targets}} \revtext{Transfer is evaluated on four semi-arid basins of north-central Chile (27--31$^\circ$S). The hyper-arid-to-humid gradient is present in the source pool, not in the target set; transfer to humid, Patagonian or extra-Andean basins is untested, and the conclusions are stated for the evaluated targets.}

\paragraph{\revtext{Feature selection in the submitted version}} \revtext{The originally submitted benchmark selected features on all 14 basins, so target labels influenced the feature space. We corrected this to source-only selection and quantified the effect (Section~\ref{sec:results:leakage}); the correction removed two features with genuine within-target signal, lowered absolute $F_1$ by 1--10~pp at $K=1$ and halved the headline advantage. We report both runs in Table~\ref{tab:cfs_comparison}.}

\paragraph{\revtext{Replication structure}} \revtext{Seeds do not generate independent spatial partitions: K-means on coordinates returns identical partitions in Huasco and strongly overlapping ones elsewhere (ARI 0.70--0.93). Inference is therefore reported at the partition level and, as a sensitivity, at the fold level, where no per-basin test can be significant with five folds; the joint test is the primary criterion. In addition, the between-run variance of meta-training ($+4.3$ vs.\ $+2.2$~pp joint advantage in two runs of the same protocol) is not captured by partition-level intervals, which condition on the trained meta-models; more seeds, or a bootstrap over meta-models, would be needed to include it. The magnitude also depends on preprocessing: one-hot encoding the categorical landform feature halves the three-seed estimate of the $K=1$ advantage (Section~\ref{sec:results:preproc}).}

\paragraph{\revtext{Selection by excluded partitions}} \revtext{Held-out clusters without both classes are skipped; the excluded clusters are the low-relief, event-free parts of Huasco and Limar\'i (Table~\ref{tab:fold_diagnostics}). The benchmark therefore describes performance in the event-bearing mountainous terrain of each basin, where $F_1$ is defined, and is silent about the flat terrain where the models would mostly predict absence.}

\paragraph{Source domain count} With 10 source basins, the regime is at the lower end of typical few-shot benchmarks (Omniglot uses 1{,}623 classes, miniImageNet uses 64 source classes), and one polygon inventory (Taltal) supplies 49\% of the source positives. \revtext{Basin-balanced weighting (Section~\ref{sec:results:weighting}) neutralises the dominance for pooled pre-training; whether a larger and more even source pool would increase the advantage of any method is untested.} Adding basins from neighboring countries (Peru, Argentina, Bolivia, Ecuador) is a natural next step.

\paragraph{MLP backbone} The main benchmark uses a 3-layer MLP on point-wise tabular features. This is intentionally conservative: it ensures interpretability, avoids large-scale GPU requirements, and maps cleanly to the practical workflow of basin managers. The U-Net-style CNN baseline of Section~\ref{sec:results:cnn} quantifies the cost of this choice on Huasco: the spatial CNN improves $F_1$ by 6--11~pp across methods and $K$ values, and the meta-learning lift is partially absorbed by the spatial representation. The MLP results should therefore be read as a conservative lower bound on achievable accuracy. A multi-target U-Net meta-learning evaluation, including richer patch sizes and attention-based architectures, is the natural follow-up.

\paragraph{Static climate} \revtext{Our climate features (WorldClim 2.1 bioclimatics) represent a 1970--2000 baseline, and none of them is retained by source-only feature selection; climate therefore enters the models only through the source pool composition.} Climate-change-driven shifts in precipitation regimes are a known driver of changing landslide hazard but are absent from our model. Coupling with CMIP6 projections is straightforward in principle.

\paragraph{Probability calibration and prevalence} Two coupled caveats govern how the outputs should be read. First, the models are evaluated at a fixed $0.5$ threshold on held-out clusters whose positive share is far above operational prevalence; under the extreme class imbalance of real basins, absolute precision is $0.05$--$0.09$ (Section~\ref{sec:results:prevalence}, Figure~\ref{fig:pr_prevalence}), \revtext{and the average-precision figures are conditional on the assumed $\pi=0.05$}. Second, a model adapted on only $2K$ support samples has no calibration guarantee, so its raw sigmoid outputs are not probabilities in the frequentist sense---which is why the susceptibility maps (Figure~\ref{fig:maps}) are shown as within-basin percentiles. Both caveats point to the same operational conclusion: the framework should be used as a \emph{ranking/stratification} tool, evaluated with prevalence-independent metrics (ROC-AUC) and prevalence-conditional ones stated as such, rather than as a per-pixel binary classifier. Where a calibrated probability is required, post-hoc calibration on the $K$-shot support (Platt scaling or isotonic regression) is the natural next step, at the cost of consuming part of the already-scarce support budget.

\paragraph{Negatives generation} \revtext{Twelve of fourteen basins lack explicit negative samples}, requiring us to generate them by random sampling within the basin polygon outside a 500~m buffer of positives \revtext{(the rule of each basin is listed in Table~\ref{tab:basin_summary})}. This introduces an implicit assumption---that all unlabeled pixels are negatives---which is statistically defensible (landslide footprints are sparse) but methodologically simplistic, and non-landslide sample selection is known to affect absolute susceptibility performance \citep{abdelkader2025nonlandslide}. \revtext{Heterogeneous negative rules across source basins could also act as a domain signal.} To test whether our conclusion depends on this choice, we re-ran the $K=1$ spatial-CV benchmark on the two exemplar targets (Huasco, Copiap\'o) under three exclusion-buffer widths (500~m, 1~km, 2~km) and a slope-matched strategy that reproduces the positives' slope distribution (Table~\ref{tab:neg_sensitivity}). As expected, absolute $F_1$ shifts with the sampling scheme, but the Reptile \emph{lift} over Independent remains positive in every condition (range \revtext{$+2.0$ to $+6.8$}~pp) and, tellingly, \emph{grows} under the slope-matched scheme ($+5.5$~pp in Huasco): when the easy ``steep-versus-flat'' confound is removed, the source-learned initialisation helps more, not less, indicating the advantage is not an artifact of easy random negatives. Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning remains a more rigorous alternative to the all-unlabeled-are-negative assumption.

\paragraph{\revtext{Mixed triggers and movement types}} \revtext{The inventories combine rainfall-triggered flows (Cha\~naral, Taltal, the northern catastro basins), earthquake-triggered slides of four typologies (Maule 2010) and mixed-trigger records (Elqui, with events from 1922, 1997 and 2015), treated as one binary susceptibility class. Susceptibility, as distinct from hazard, concerns where slopes fail given static conditioning factors, and national inventories are routinely aggregated for that purpose; but a seismically triggered source inventory may encode conditioning patterns that do not transfer to rainfall-dominated targets, and the trigger of most catastro records is not recorded. Trigger-aware sampling and feature design would likely improve discrimination, particularly in Elqui, and a leave-one-source-out analysis by trigger is the appropriate next test.}

\subsection{Practical recommendations for new-basin susceptibility modeling}
\label{sec:disc:practical}

For a researcher or basin manager facing a \emph{newly-studied} watershed with fewer than 10 documented landslide events, our findings support the following protocol:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \revtext{\textbf{Start from a pooled-source model with every source basin weighted equally.} A pooled logistic regression on open DEM descriptors matched or exceeded every neural method at $K=1$ in all four targets; a basin-balanced pooled network is the appropriate neural alternative. Size-weighted pooling, in which one large inventory dominates, is the configuration to avoid.}
    \item \revtext{\textbf{Reach for a meta-learned initialisation when adaptation must happen in a few gradient steps} and a neural backbone is required; Reptile is the cheapest of the meta-learners we tested and statistically equivalent to FOMAML and ProtoNet. Expect a 2--4~pp $F_1$ advantage over training from scratch at one event per class, none beyond ten.}
    \item \textbf{Avoid adversarial domain adaptation} when source-domain count is small ($N \leq 10$); it offered no few-shot advantage here.
    \item \revtext{\textbf{Select features and estimate every data-dependent parameter on the source basins only.} Including the target labels in feature selection doubled the apparent few-shot advantage in this benchmark.}
    \item \textbf{Validate with spatial cross-validation, not random hold-out}, \revtext{report the separation between query and support pixels, and check an exclusion buffer of the order of the residual autocorrelation range: here buffers up to 10~km changed nothing and 20~km cost 0.7~pp.} Random CV inflated $F_1$ by up to 21~pp in the heterogeneous basins and by nothing in another; the \emph{relative} method ranking was preserved, but the \emph{absolute} numbers reported to stakeholders should come from spatial CV.
    \item \revtext{\textbf{Report uncertainty at the level of spatial partitions, with a pre-specified joint hypothesis}; pooled episode-level intervals were $3.3\times$ too narrow, per-basin tests with ten partitions are underpowered, and seeds do not create independent partitions.}
    \item \textbf{Adapt for $\leq 30$ gradient steps}. Source-learned initializations reach near-peak $F_1$ within 1--3 steps; further adaptation provides diminishing returns and risks over-fitting on the small support set.
    \item \textbf{Document hard cases}. Basins with strong intra-basin heterogeneity (multiple lithologies, multiple triggers, strong elevation gradient) may resist transfer and require trigger-stratified or lithology-stratified analysis as a follow-up.
\end{enumerate}
